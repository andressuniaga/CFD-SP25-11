\section{Introduction}
Flow around a circular cylinder is a classic problem in \textit{fluid mechanics} and is commonly studied in \textit{computational fluid dynamics} (CFD) because of its interesting flow behavior at different Reynolds numbers (Re). At low Re, the flow is steady and symmetric, but as Re increases, the wake behind the cylinder becomes unsteady, first generating recirculation regions and ultimately leading to \textit{vortex shedding} and the formation of a \textit{von K\'arm\'an vortex street}. This type of flow is important in many engineering applications where understanding how vortices form and interact with surfaces can help improve designs.
\vspace{2.5mm}

The goal of this project is to \textbf{simulate flow past a circular cylinder using OpenFOAM} and analyze how the flow changes at different Reynolds numbers. Specifically, we will:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Generate and refine structured meshes} using \texttt{blockMesh} to see how grid resolution affects accuracy.
    \item \textbf{Simulate steady and unsteady flow} at \(Re = 20\) (where flow remains steady) and \(Re = 110\) (where vortex shedding occurs).
    \item \textbf{Analyze velocity, pressure, and forces}, including shear stress and drag.
    \item \textbf{Validate our results} by comparing vortex shedding frequency (Strouhal number) and separation point locations with published data from \textit{Williamson (1988)} and \textit{Park et al. (1998)}.
\end{itemize}

The simulations will be performed using the \textbf{OpenFOAM}, for \textit{incompressible, laminar flow}. The computational setup includes \textbf{mesh generation, a no-slip boundary on the cylinder, and downstream data probes}. All variables are nondimensionalized using the cylinder diameter \((D = 1 \text{ m})\) and freestream velocity \((U = 1 \text{ m/s})\), which simplifies the Reynolds number to:
\begin{equation}
    Re = \frac{U D}{\nu} = \frac{1}{\nu}.
\end{equation}


In this report, we will go through the \textbf{mesh setup, simulation process, and post-processing results using ParaView} to understand how the flow behaves at different Reynolds numbers. By refining our meshes and analyzing key flow parameters, we aim to gain a better understanding of \textit{how OpenFOAM handles external flow simulations and how mesh quality affects the results}.

\section{Unsteady Flow: Strouhal Number of the vortex shedding}

The Strouhal number (\( St \)) characterizes the vortex shedding frequency in unsteady flow past a circular cylinder. It is defined as:

\begin{equation*}
    St = \frac{f D}{U}
\end{equation*}

where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \( f \) is the frequency of vortex shedding,
    \item \( D \) is the cylinder diameter,
    \item \( U \) is the free-stream velocity.
\end{itemize}

The Strouhal number is a key parameter in predicting vortex-induced vibrations, which are critical in the design of bridges, underwater pipelines, and aircraft structures. Accurately predicting \( St \) allows engineers to prevent structural resonance and optimize aerodynamic performance.

\subsection{Methodology}
To determine the vortex shedding frequency, the following procedure was used:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A velocity probe was placed at coordinates **(2.0, -0.5, 0.0)** downstream of the cylinder to capture unsteady velocity signals.
    \item The velocity signal was recorded using OpenFOAM’s **probes** function.
    \item The last **10 seconds** of the simulation, where vortex shedding was most stable, were analyzed.
    \item A **Fast Fourier Transform (FFT)** was applied to the velocity signal to extract the dominant frequency.
    \item Simulations were performed on different meshes and time steps to ensure results were mesh- and time-step-independent.
    \item To ensure the computed Strouhal number is independent of numerical settings,  
    we tested different mesh resolutions and time step sizes, as summarized in Table~\ref{tab:st_results}.  
    Minimal variation in \( St \) across these cases confirms the robustness of our solution.    
\end{itemize}

Mesh convergence was verified by visualizing the solution in **ParaView** using the `.foam` file and ensuring that vortex shedding patterns remained consistent across different resolutions. This process also guided the choice of probe placement to ensure it captured the periodic shedding without excessive numerical diffusion.

\subsection{Simulation Setup}
The simulations were performed using OpenFOAM’s \texttt{icoFoam} solver for incompressible, laminar flow. A second-order accurate time discretization scheme was used. The time step was dynamically adjusted to maintain a Courant number \( Co \leq 0.5 \). The results for different mesh configurations and time steps are summarized below:

By refining **\(\Delta t\), mesh resolution, and numerical schemes**, future simulations can achieve better agreement with experimental data.

\subsection{Rate of Strain Tensor Calculation}
The rate of strain tensor \( S_{ij} \) describes the velocity gradient contributions to fluid deformation and is defined as:
\begin{equation*}
S_{ij} = \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} + \frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i} \right)
\end{equation*}
By plotting \( S_{ij} \) as a function of \( 1/\delta \), we can analyze how the boundary layer thickness influences strain rates. The computed strain rate distribution is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:strain_tensor}.
